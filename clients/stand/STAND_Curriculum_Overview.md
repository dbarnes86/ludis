# STAND Curriculum Overview
**Status:** Working document — Dan Barnes / Ludis Management Ltd
**Date:** May 2026

---

## What's been built and prototyped

Two modules have working prototypes in the Ludis workspace:

**Module 05 — Find Your Customer** (v1 + v2)
Who the business is for. Kid builds a customer card, names one specific person, understands their budget, and writes a promise statement. Output feeds into the parent proof sheet.

**Module 06 — Your Launch Plan** (v5 + v6, just deployed)
3-act structure: define the customer, map where to find them, build a launch kit (text message, flyer, IG story, business card). Word-of-mouth first, always.

The product itself has **Perfect Your Pitch** built and live — voice-coached, kid practises out loud. That's sitting correctly at the start of the lesson carousel as the first thing a new business owner does after setup.

Everything else below is how I'm thinking about the remaining modules.

---

## The overall structure

The curriculum runs across two phases:

**LAUNCH** — Modules 01–03. These are foundational. A kid can't grow a business they haven't properly set up. The goal of this phase is to make the business feel real and defined before any selling happens.

**GROW** — Modules 04–06. These are operational. The business exists; now the kid learns to run it. This phase connects directly to the dashboard and real sales activity.

**LEAD** — Module 08 (CEO Review). This is a capstone, not a lesson. The kid looks back at what they built and makes a decision about where to take it next.

The pitch lesson (Perfect Your Pitch) acts as a bridge between onboarding and the curriculum — it's the first thing that appears on the dashboard after the business is built, and correctly so. It converts the abstract thing they just created into something they can actually say out loud to another person.

## Module-by-module thinking

### Module 01 — Your Customer
**Phase:** Launch
**Status:** Prototyped (v1 + v2 in workspace)
**What the kid does:** Names one specific customer. Describes what that person wants, what they're worried about spending money on, and what would make them choose this business over doing nothing. Writes a one-sentence promise.
**What they come away with:** A customer card. This is a real output — it feeds into the parent proof sheet and informs everything downstream.
**Design note:** The prompt that worked best in prototyping was making the customer concrete and personal ("a mum at the school gate") rather than categorical ("parents"). Generic customer = generic marketing. The module should push hard on specificity.

---

### Module 02 — Price It Right
**Phase:** Launch
**Status:** Coming Soon in product
**What the kid does:** Calculates their actual cost (materials + time), sets a retail price, understands profit per sale. Works toward a sales goal — how many sales to hit a target amount.
**What they come away with:** A pricing card showing cost / price / profit. A sales goal (e.g. "sell 10 units to earn £50").
**Design note:** This needs to be grounded in the kid's actual business type — the numbers for stickers look very different from baked goods. The profit per sale figure shown in the ideation flow ($8.49 for a cupcake stand) is the right hook. Make it feel achievable, not abstract. The goal-setting element is what makes this sticky — it gives the kid a reason to check the dashboard.

---

### Module 03 — Your Brand Story
**Phase:** Launch
**Status:** Not in the lesson carousel yet (Brand Studio covers brand visuals in onboarding, but the story/mission layer isn't there)
**What the kid does:** Writes a short mission statement. Answers: what does this business do, who is it for, and why does it exist. Gets a one-sentence version they can say out loud and a two-line version for their storefront.
**What they come away with:** Brand story card. Mission statement. This feeds into Megaphone/marketing and the storefront about section.
**Design note:** This is the module most at risk of feeling like a school exercise. The framing needs to be about what makes the business theirs — not "write your mission statement" but "what would make a stranger trust you in 5 seconds?" The story has to feel personal. If Brand Studio already captures some of this, this module can be shorter — it's filling in the words layer that sits on top of the visual brand.

---

### Module 04 — Social Media 101
**Phase:** Grow
**Status:** Coming Soon in product
**What the kid does:** Chooses one platform to focus on (not all of them — one). Learns what to post, how often, and what makes people follow a business. Writes their first three posts.
**What they come away with:** Three drafted posts (or captions) ready to go. A posting rhythm — what to say when you have nothing to sell and when you do.
**Design note:** The big design decision here is whether to push toward a specific platform or stay generic. My recommendation is to build the module around the concept (here's what good content does for a business) with light platform-specific flavouring, rather than building a TikTok module and an Instagram module. The mechanics are the same — the kid needs to understand what they're selling in each post, even when they're not explicitly selling. "Show your process" is the most accessible starting point for most business types.

### Module 07 — Land Your First Customer
**Phase:** Grow
**Status:** Coming Soon in product
**What the kid does:** Builds a list of 10 real people who might buy. Writes a specific message for each type of person (friend vs. stranger). Practises asking for the sale without it feeling awkward. Sets a target: first sale within X days.
**What they come away with:** An outreach list. A first message they're actually willing to send. A deadline they set themselves.
**Design note:** This is the hardest module emotionally — asking someone to buy something is scary for most kids. The framing needs to normalise the "no." Rejection isn't failure; it's information. The module should teach that most sales come from follow-up, not the first ask. The voice pitch element already built (Perfect Your Pitch) is the rehearsal; this module is the actual game.

---

### Module 08 — Track Your Money
**Phase:** Lead
**Status:** Coming Soon in product
**What the kid does:** Records their first sales. Understands what they've earned vs. what they've spent. Looks at whether the business made a profit. Makes a decision: reinvest into more materials, or pocket the money.
**What they come away with:** A simple profit/loss snapshot. A decision — reinvest or pocket — and a reason they chose it.
**Design note:** Money Math in onboarding covers the unit economics. This module is the real-world check-in once they've actually sold something. The key emotional beat is the first time they see a real profit figure — even if it's small. That moment needs to be celebrated visibly. The dashboard metrics exist for this. The reinvest/pocket decision is a real business judgment and the module should treat it like one.

---

### Module 09 — Think Like an Inventor
**Phase:** Lead
**Status:** Coming Soon in product
**What the kid does:** Looks at their business with fresh eyes. Asks: what's selling, what isn't, what would a new product look like? Brainstorms one new product or improvement. Runs a basic test (tell 3 people, see what they say).
**What they come away with:** One new product idea with a short rationale. A testing habit — ask before you build.
**Design note:** This is the iteration module. The whole product is built around the idea that entrepreneurship is a cycle, not a one-time event. This module closes that loop explicitly. The best framing for kids is: "What would you change if you were starting again?" That question is accessible at any age and produces genuinely useful thinking.

---

### Module 10 — CEO Review (Capstone)
**Phase:** Lead
**Status:** Not in product yet
**What the kid does:** Reviews the business from start to now. Looks at what they built, what they sold, what worked, what they'd do differently. Gets a summary card — their business story in one page.
**What they come away with:** A CEO Report Card. This is a shareable, printable output — designed to go on a wall or be shown to a parent/teacher. It's the graduation moment.
**Design note:** This should feel like an event, not a lesson. The product already does "Alice, you just built a real business" at the end of onboarding — this is the grown-up version of that moment, after the kid has actually run the business for a while. It also resets the loop — the CEO Review ends with the question "what's next?" which opens the door to the next business or the next phase of this one.

## What I'd prioritise building next

The two modules with the clearest upstream/downstream dependencies are **Price It Right (02)** and **Land Your First Customer (07)**. Price It Right because nothing else makes sense without understanding the money. Land Your First Customer because it's the bridge between the product existing and the kid actually making a sale — and first sale is the single most important metric in the engagement loop.

Social Media 101 and Track Your Money are important but they're more self-contained — they don't block other things.

The CEO Review (10) should be scoped last, but the data model for it should be thought about early — it needs to aggregate outputs from every other module.
